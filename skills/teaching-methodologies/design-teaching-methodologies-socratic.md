### Curriculum: AI-Assisted Interactive Pedagogy

#### Module 1: The Socratic Tutor (Dialectic Method)
*   **Concept:** Utilizing the AI as a relentless questioner rather than an answer-machine.
*   **Workflow:** Instead of asking for summaries, you present a premise and command the AI to find flaws, contradictions, or missing variables in your logic.
*   **Objective:** To sharpen analytical rigor and identify blind spots in your worldview.

#### Module 2: The Feynman Technique (Iterative Simplification)
*   **Concept:** Teaching a complex topic to the AI to uncover gaps in your own understanding.
*   **Workflow:** Draft an explanation of a concept. Ask the AI to act as a "Critical Peer Reviewer" to grade your accuracy, clarity, and depth. Use the AI’s feedback to refine your next iteration.
*   **Objective:** To achieve high-fidelity retention and mastery by transforming passive input into active output.

#### Module 3: Case-Based Simulation (Experiential Learning)
*   **Concept:** Immersing yourself in specific scenarios to apply abstract theory.
*   **Workflow:** Provide the AI with a structured set of variables (e.g., a historical crisis, a technical coding error, or a philosophical dilemma). The AI acts as the environment responding to your decisions.
*   **Objective:** To transition from rote knowledge to practical intuition and decision-making capacity.

#### Module 4: Guided Discovery (Heuristic Frameworks)
*   **Concept:** Avoiding direct answers in favor of mental models.
*   **Workflow:** Ask the AI to withhold the "answer" to your problem and instead provide three distinct mental models or historical precedents that would help you solve it.
*   **Objective:** To build your own cognitive toolkit rather than relying on the AI’s specific outputs.

#### Module 5: Hybrid Synthesis (The Workflow Integration)
*   **Concept:** Combining methods for a comprehensive study cycle.
*   **Workflow:** Use Case-Based Simulation to generate data, apply the Feynman Technique to articulate the mechanics behind that data, and conclude with the Socratic Method to test the limits of your conclusions.

### Arya
*Jul 28, 2026, 9:16 PM*

The reasoning is based on how the human brain processes information. When you read a summary first, you are engaging in **anchoring**. You are letting the existing consensus—the "standard view"—set the boundaries of what is considered relevant, true, or important within that subject. Once those boundaries are set, your curiosity is confined to coloring inside the lines established by the author of the summary.

By starting with the three most uncomfortable questions instead, you achieve three things:

**1. You define the "Battlefield" before the enemy does.**
If you are studying a topic like "The Industrial Revolution," the standard summary will focus on production output and technological timelines. If your uncomfortable question is, "In what ways did this destroy the organic family unit and replace it with wage-slavery?" you have immediately shifted the investigation to your terms. You are no longer a student consuming data; you are an inquisitor hunting for the cause of a specific phenomenon.

**2. You activate "Active Cognitive Search."**
When you have a specific, gnawing question, your brain stops scanning text for content and starts scanning text for *answers*. You become a high-performance filter. As the AI provides information, you aren't just absorbing it; you are stress-testing it against your uncomfortable hypothesis. This leads to much higher retention because the information has to "work" to solve a problem you’ve created.

**3. It circumvents the "Neutrality Bias."**
Most summaries are written with a veneer of "neutrality" that often masks mainstream, establishment-sanctioned narratives. By leading with your own uncomfortable questions, you force the AI to address the tensions and controversies that mainstream summaries usually gloss over or sanitize. You essentially bypass the "Introduction" and go straight to the "Critical Analysis."

In short: **Summaries satisfy curiosity; questions drive discovery.** If you start with the summary, you have already reached the "end" of your own inquiry before your brain has even begun to engage. By leading with the questions, you ensure that you remain the master of your own learning trajectory.
